6 Design Rationale & Development Process

Research Question

How do cold cloud-top regions change in GOES-19 Channel 13 imagery over a 3-hour window, and what do these changes suggest about short-term convective activity? GOES-19 Channel 13 (10.3 µm thermal infrared) measures emitted radiation from cloud tops or the surface. Colder brightness temperatures often correspond to higher cloud tops and deeper convection, while warmer values often correspond to lower clouds or the surface.

What We Learned From The Visualization

Over 18:01-20:56 UTC on May 5, 2026, cold cloud-top regions in the CONUS scene expanded in extent, intensified in depth, and became more spatially heterogeneous. This pattern is consistent with afternoon convective development: surface heating drives updrafts, which push cloud tops higher and colder as the afternoon progresses.

Dataset

NOAA GOES-19 ABI Level-2 Cloud and Moisture Imagery (CMIPC), Channel 13, CONUS domain. 36 frames at 5-minute intervals, 18:01–20:56 UTC, May 5 2026. Downloaded from the NOAA GOES Open Data Registry on AWS (s3://noaa-goes19/ABI-L2-CMIPC/2026/125/{18,19,20}/, public bucket, no credentials required). Raw NetCDF4 files were processed by scripts/process_goes_to_png.py: the CMI variable (brightness temperature array) was normalized to a shared global min/max range across all 36 frames and exported as grayscale PNGs. Per-frame statistics (mean, min, max, fraction of pixels below 240 K as a cold cloud proxy, and standard deviation) were written to data/frames/cloud_summary.csv.

Visual Encodings

Position (y-axis) encodes brightness temperature in Kelvin throughout all chart views, chosen as the most accurate quantitative channel for comparison. In the satellite image, luminance encodes temperature: brighter pixels are colder, matching the standard meteorological convention for thermal IR imagery. In the scatter plot, hue encodes time (frame index), using D3's interpolateCool scale so temporal progression is readable without obscuring the position encoding. Dot size is fixed to avoid conflating area with a variable that has no area meaning here. The standard deviation chart uses a filled area + line to encode scene temperature heterogeneity over time. Std rises when isolated cold convective towers form amid a warm background, a signal not visible in the mean or min/max charts. Position on the y-axis encodes Kelvin; the warm yellow color distinguishes it visually from the blue mean and red min lines in adjacent views.

Interaction Design

All five views are linked through a single setFrame() function. Clicking any dot on the mean chart, scatter plot, or bar chart jumps the satellite image and all other views to that frame simultaneously, using a details-on-demand technique that lets users connect a statistical anomaly directly to the spatial imagery that caused it. The scatter plot's orange highlight ring and the bar chart's highlight box both move to the selected frame, maintaining positional context across views. The play animation with adjustable speed lets users observe temporal evolution across all views at once, which a static plot cannot support.

Data Transformations

Each frame's mean, min, and max CMI values are scene-level aggregations, computed over all valid (finite, non-fill) pixels in the CONUS domain for that 5-minute scan. The global min (~198.39 K) and max (~329.96 K) are computed across all 36 frames combined and used to normalize the PNG grayscale values so that brightness is consistent across frames. No data was filtered or excluded beyond masking fill values as defined in the GOES-R product specification.

Development Process

We spend about 20-25 hours total across the team. Setting up the data pipeline (process_goes_to_png.py) to pull NetCDF4 files from AWS S3 is the hardest part, since the data couldn't be download like the typicle csv file. Then we Handle the GOES-R ABI fixed-grid projection, and compute per-frame statistics took the second most time due to coordinate system issues (~5 hours). Building the D3 visualization with five linked views took ~12 hours, as we were having a hard time making the thermal graph colorful. After that, the scatter plot and std deviation chart being the most insightful additions since they reveal patterns invisible in the mean line chart alone. Writeup and final polish took ~3 hours.
